# Choosing a visual direction

Read for new interfaces and substantial visual redesigns. These are decision aids, not a mandatory house style. The user's reference and existing product conventions take priority.

## Start from the subject

Describe what the interface helps someone do and what makes this product specific. Translate that into composition, density, imagery and tone. “Premium”, “modern” and “clean” are not enough to choose a design.

When direction is open, consider two plausible approaches briefly and choose the one that best supports the task. Do not produce several full implementations or a mood-board deliverable unless requested. If browsing is available and references would resolve uncertainty, inspect one or two relevant primary sites or supplied images. Record the useful principle, not a copied brand, unlicensed asset or invented claim about what was viewed. Proceed from local context when browsing is unavailable.

Examples of genuinely different decisions, not templates to reuse:

| Product need | Composition worth considering | Evidence to prioritize |
|---|---|---|
| Editorial or cultural project | Strong reading sequence, expressive type, varied image scale | Readability, narrative, meaningful content |
| Operational workspace | Compact task area, aligned data, predictable navigation | Scanning, comparison, states, keyboard use |
| Physical product or place | Product material, scale and detail in imagery | Accurate assets, purchase/visit flow |
| Creative portfolio | Work samples with intentional pacing | Credible work, clear authorship and contact |

## Make the decisions visible

- **Composition:** choose what gets attention first and why. Use grouping, alignment and relative size before ornamental containers. Let content determine section length and rhythm. Tables, lists, open layouts and cards all have legitimate uses.
- **Type:** select roles, not a random font collection. Test the headline with actual words and line breaks; keep body and control text comfortable. Match fonts to the brand and language, including Cyrillic when needed. Existing system fonts are valid; do not ban a family to force novelty. Prefer already available fonts; handle fallback and loading without layout surprises.
- **Color:** assign roles for action, status, emphasis and surfaces. Check the actual foreground/background pairs. Do not replace a brand's purple, dark theme or gradients merely because similar choices appear in generic generated pages.
- **Assets:** use an image, product view, diagram, illustration or typographic treatment that explains the subject. Choose a focal point and crop for narrow screens. Avoid unrelated stock filler and fragile remote image URLs. Check usage rights before importing third-party assets.
- **Rhythm:** vary density where the content warrants it. A page should not repeat the same three-column grid for every section. In an app, consistency can be more useful than expressive variation.
- **Interaction:** make the primary task obvious and the result understandable. Choose motion only where it improves the experience. Do not install an animation library for a hover fade or require a quota of effects.

## Diagnose a generic result

Ask whether changing the brand name would leave an equally plausible site for an unrelated industry. If so, find what is missing from the product's content or composition. Change that decision instead of adding more decoration.

Repeated rounded panels, random gradient blobs, giant empty heroes, ornamental numbers, excessive badges and placeholder copy are warning signs when they have no informational purpose. They are not universal prohibitions. A branded purple dashboard with necessary cards can be the correct result.

Do not force a promotional hero into an operational app, sparsify useful dense data to look expensive, or add cursor effects/scroll hijacking to manufacture personality. Polish comes from legible hierarchy, credible content, fit and finish, and a working task flow.
